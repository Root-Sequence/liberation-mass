# Liberation Mass — Spatial Principles

Liberation Mass is not only a sequence of activities that happens to occur somewhere.

**The physical space is part of the practice.**

The website, documents, schedules, maps, and any future Community Infrastructure integrations should **translate and support the space rather than define it**.

> **Physical space is the source of truth; digital systems are interfaces into it.**

This does not mean digital participation is lesser or that every person must be physically present. It means the spatial design of a physical gathering cannot be reduced to a software taxonomy.

---

## 1. Many spaces, no compulsory path

Liberation Mass already describes an ecosystem of Healing, Growth, Being, Commons, Play, Mutual Aid, Creation, Spiritual-but-Not, Kids & Families, Food & Nourishment, Conflict & Mediation, and Archive spaces.

These should function as **affordances**, not stations on a mandatory route.

People should be able to:

- enter where appropriate;
- leave a space without explanation;
- move between spaces;
- stay in one space;
- sit at the edge;
- observe;
- participate partially;
- return later;
- go outside;
- leave the gathering.

No one should need to complete “the Mass experience.”

---

## 2. Rest belongs everywhere

Rest should not be confined to one designated accessibility or quiet area.

Where possible, distribute:

- chairs;
- benches;
- cushions;
- leaning/perching options;
- reading areas;
- water;
- shade/weather protection;
- places to pause without being interpreted as disengaged.

A person should not have to disclose a disability, pain, fatigue, age, anxiety, sensory need, or reason in order to sit down.

The **Being Space** remains a low-sensory sanctuary, but the rest of the gathering should not become physically hostile merely because a dedicated quiet room exists.

---

## 3. Spatial dignity

Different spaces can have different energy without becoming a hierarchy of worth.

A person dancing in Play Space is not more engaged than someone sitting silently in Being Space.

A person working on a campaign in Commons Space is not performing more valuable participation than someone eating, repairing a coat, talking with one other person, reading a zine, or resting.

Spatial design can embody the principle:

> **No compulsory performance of participation.**

---

## 4. Access before arrival

Someone should be able to learn enough about the space before deciding whether to attend.

Useful public information may include:

- step-free access;
- entrances;
- elevators;
- accessible restrooms;
- seating/rest availability;
- quiet/low-sensory areas;
- scent/noise considerations where known;
- outdoor/indoor areas;
- childcare/family space;
- transportation options;
- bicycle/adaptive-cycle parking;
- drop-off/loading access;
- cost or no-cost participation;
- whether someone can arrive late or leave early;
- whether someone can come alone;
- whether someone can simply observe;
- contact point for access questions.

This connects directly to Community Infrastructure's **Discovery Profile** model.

---

## 5. Getting there is part of accessibility

A fully accessible room is not meaningfully accessible if the route to it is impossible.

Hosts should consider:

- transit service and service hours;
- paratransit/drop-off access;
- walking/rolling conditions;
- safe bicycle/adaptive-cycle access;
- parking where genuinely needed;
- rides or accompaniment;
- lighting and nighttime departure;
- whether the gathering ends after transit stops running;
- weather;
- distance between modes and the entrance;
- remote participation when appropriate.

Community Infrastructure could eventually support scoped ride/accompaniment Needs ↔ Offers without making transportation coordination a prerequisite for hosting a Mass.

---

## 6. Spatial discoverability

A person may discover Liberation Mass through a place or activity before understanding the project as a whole.

Examples:

- public repair table;
- free store;
- community meal;
- dance event;
- skillshare;
- quiet reading/zine area;
- community garden activity;
- open Commons meeting.

That is legitimate.

Someone should not need to accept an entire identity, worldview, or organizational membership before participating in one useful or meaningful activity.

The place can be a pathway into Community.

---

## 7. The space should explain itself

Good spatial design reduces the amount of insider knowledge required to participate.

Possible tools include:

- clear physical signage;
- simple maps;
- color/symbol systems with text equivalents;
- visible access routes;
- clearly marked exits;
- readable schedules;
- obvious water/restroom locations;
- people available to orient newcomers;
- printed information;
- QR/website information as an optional layer, not the only layer.

A person without a phone should still be able to understand where they are and what is happening.

---

## 8. Digital systems should not surveil the gathering

Liberation Mass should not require cameras, face recognition, participant tracking, or stored movement histories in order to understand how spaces are being used.

If environmental sensing is ever used for practical building functions, it should avoid identifying, categorizing, or storing individual movement wherever possible.

Useful questions can often be answered through:

- steward observation;
- voluntary feedback;
- aggregate headcounts where needed;
- environmental measurements;
- explicit access reports;
- post-event reflection.

The gathering is not an analytics funnel.

---

## 9. Space as commons

A Mass may occur in:

- living rooms;
- parks;
- halls;
- community centers;
- gardens;
- warehouses;
- public plazas;
- borrowed institutional space;
- purpose-built spaces;
- temporary pop-ups.

Different ownership models create different constraints.

Questions include:

- Who can enter?
- Who controls hours?
- Who can exclude someone?
- What costs must be recovered?
- Can the space be altered?
- Who performs cleanup and maintenance?
- Who stores shared Resources?
- What happens when the venue relationship ends?

The social ideal of a commons does not erase leases, access rules, building codes, neighbors, maintenance, or material limits.

Those constraints should remain visible.

---

## 10. Space and stewardship

Spatial stewardship is responsibility for conditions, not ownership of the people inside them.

Possible responsibilities:

- keeping exits clear;
- maintaining accessibility routes;
- managing supplies;
- supporting quiet/sensory needs;
- signage/orientation;
- de-escalation;
- cleaning;
- opening/closing;
- checking bathrooms/water;
- helping with rearrangement;
- receiving access feedback.

A space steward does not gain general authority over participants merely by caring for the space.

---

## 11. The gathering should be physically remixable

Spaces should be capable of local adaptation.

Examples:

- move furniture;
- create a temporary quieter area;
- expand seating;
- move an activity outdoors;
- lower sound;
- open another food table;
- split a crowded room;
- create a new space when a recurring need appears.

The spatial model is modular precisely because communities differ.

A permanent floorplan should not become doctrine.

---

## 12. Distributed knowledge and media

Books, zines, guides, devices, and other community knowledge do not need to live only in Archive Space.

They can appear ambiently throughout the gathering:

- reading materials near seating;
- repair guides near Creation Space;
- consent/access cards near entrances;
- local resource information near Mutual Aid Space;
- small libraries in Being/Growth areas;
- open/community-controlled digital devices where useful.

Knowledge should feel present in the space rather than locked behind one information desk.

---

## 13. Spatial maintenance is part of the ritual

Setup, cleaning, repair, accessibility checks, furniture movement, food service, cable management, trash/compost, supply replenishment, and closing are not invisible backstage details.

They are part of what makes the gathering possible.

This does not mean everyone must perform every task.

It means the project should not narrate the gathering as if care and culture appear magically while maintenance labor happens offscreen.

---

## 14. Community Infrastructure boundary

Community Infrastructure may support:

- discovery;
- access notes;
- maps/lists;
- ride/accompaniment coordination;
- shared Resources;
- schedules;
- roles;
- Community Knowledge;
- feedback;
- federated node discovery;
- low-tech/print outputs.

But:

> **The database is not the room.**

A digital representation will always omit atmosphere, crowding, smell, weather, social dynamics, improvisation, interpersonal care, and other embodied conditions.

Hosts and participants remain authoritative about what is actually happening in the space.

---

## 15. Spatial review questions

Before a gathering, ask:

- Can someone understand the access conditions before arriving?
- Can they realistically get here and get home?
- Can someone come alone?
- Can someone participate without standing?
- Can someone participate without speaking?
- Is rest distributed or segregated?
- Can someone leave without navigating social permission?
- Does a newcomer need a smartphone?
- Are directions/signage understandable?
- Are sensory conditions described honestly?
- Are public and sensitive spaces appropriately separated?
- Who is doing spatial maintenance?
- What happens if the elevator, transit, power, network, or weather fails?
- What would make the space more usable next time?

---

## Working principle

> **Liberation Mass should be inhabitable, not merely attendable.**

The aim is not to move participants efficiently through a program.

It is to create space in which different people can arrive, orient, rest, participate, move, remain, connect, and leave with dignity.
